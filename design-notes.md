# Mind-Merged Robot Game: Design Notes

Working notes for a possible reimplementation of a 1985 home-computer game (Martech's tie-in game, played by the author on the Amstrad CPC), with all licensed branding removed. Target: a modern Windows laptop.

Status: research and design discussion. Nothing built yet.

**How to read this document.** Every statement is tagged by where it comes from:
- **[Manual]** the original instruction manual (Spectrum and Amstrad, 1985), supplied by the author.
- **[Author-observed]** what the author saw or remembers from playing the Amstrad version.
- **[Reviews]** period magazine reviews (mostly Spectrum and C64 versions).
- **[Author-direction]** something the author has said they want.
- **[Proposal]** a suggestion from the design discussion that has not been confirmed.

Original names (robots, places, enemies) are not used here; role names are used instead. Our game needs its own names.

---

## 1. Goal and constraints

- Re-create the *feel and structure* of the original as a standalone game with its own name, world, art and audio. [Author-direction]
- Must be good enough to be worth playing in its own right; the author has already finished the original recently, so this is not nostalgia-only. [Author-direction]
- Legal posture (not legal advice): reuse ideas and mechanics; do not reuse names, robot designs, original art or code, music, map artwork, or exact UI layouts. Pick a new name and setting.

## 2. The original, as established

### 2.1 Versions and sources
- Released 1985-86 for Spectrum, Amstrad CPC, C64 and MSX. The manual covers Spectrum and Amstrad together, so those two are treated as the same design.
- **The C64 version is a different game in several ways** [Reviews]: eight networks and eight pieces, music by Rob Hubbard, and extra subsystems (rail gun, jamming, rotating shields, missile fuel loading, a manipulate icon). This explains why the author does not remember jamming or shields from the Amstrad version: **they were C64 features, not Amstrad ones.** Earlier notes in this document that mixed them in have been corrected.

### 2.2 Premise [Manual]
The player is a human who has merged minds with a small, fast robot and sees nothing of the outside world. Everything the player sees is "projected" by the robot as images, like thoughts popping into the mind. The robot's primary drive is survival: if it feels threatened, its own feelings affect the decisions it takes. The top display (the "thought" trace) shows the robot's state of mind and can be interpreted with experience; this is probably the spiky trace at the top left of the dashboard.

### 2.3 Goal and progression [Manual]
- Recover six pieces of a supreme combat robot, rebuild it, merge with it, then find and destroy the enemy leader.
- The pieces are buried under six different city domes, with at most one piece per network. Four networks therefore have no piece at all. **Which domes hold pieces may be different every time the game is played.**
- To find a piece: destroy the dome, move into the destroyed city, and use the scanner to see whether a piece is there. Pieces are too large to carry, so the player calls a spacecraft by radio to collect each one. Every collection upgrades the player's robot, which becomes larger and more powerful.
- Six robot forms are listed in ascending order of power, from the small fast starting form up to the final supreme robot.

### 2.4 The world [Manual, plus Author-observed drawn map]
- Ten numbered city networks, each in its own valley. Mountain ranges separate the networks, which are connected by relatively passable valleys. The player starts in a valley between two impassable ranges.
- Each network has **eight city domes, one power plant, one mine and one beacon** (so about 80 domes in total, of which only six hold anything).
- Terrain tiers (valleys, higher ground, hills) exist on the map. [Author-observed] They do nothing except block movement; they do not even block missiles (possibly a bug).
- The map is effectively a graph: ten nodes joined by narrow passes. This makes reachability easy to validate in a generator.

### 2.5 Buildings [Manual]
- **City domes** are construction plants where the androids build new enemy robots. Each dome has a force field that protects it from missile attack.
- **Power plant**: supplies the power the enemy robots need, and is connected to every dome, powering the force fields. It has no protective field of its own, but when attacked it switches its power to an extremely strong deflector shield. That temporarily drains the network's power (so the dome force fields go down), but **the power plant itself survives.** [Author-observed] The window in which domes are vulnerable lasts roughly 45 seconds. (The 45 seconds is the author's guess, not from the original; see section 4.3.)
- **Mine**: provides the raw materials for enemy robot production.
- **Beacon**: sends a continuous signal that alerts the two boss robots to any intruder in that network. [Author-observed] In practice it only alerted the two bosses, which mostly made them easier to find in the endgame.
- Each type has its own map icon and its own line art in the missile terminal phase. [Author-observed]
- What each type drops when destroyed is not described in the manual. [Author-observed] Upgrades are always under buildings, but not every building holds one. Nothing is "just there" on the map. Drops come only from destroyed enemies and buildings.

### 2.6 Enemies [Manual, plus Author-observed]
Six enemy types in ascending order of power, described by role:
| Role | Behaviour | Replaced when destroyed? |
|---|---|---|
| Fast patroller | Patrols important locations; defensive guns only, no missiles; when its location is attacked it is sent at speed to bring help, usually the defenders | Yes, by a working dome |
| Hauler | Travels between mines, power plants and domes carrying raw material and power pods; if destroyed, the cargo is not damaged and can be picked up with the scanner | Yes, by a working dome |
| Defender | Kept on stand-by inside domes; attacks intruders without mercy when alerted | Yes, by a working dome |
| Hunter | One per network; set patrols looking for intruders; guns and missiles | No |
| Boss 1 | Defected renegade; heavily armoured; has radar that detects beacon signals; roams between networks | No |
| Boss 2 | The enemy leader; roams between networks; heads straight for the player when a beacon alerts him | No |
- **Replacement is a supply chain:** destroyed patrollers, haulers and defenders are replaced by *correctly functioning domes*, meaning domes with enough power and raw material. This is already a built-in limiter on enemy numbers. [Manual]
- [Author-observed] Enemies are roughly two kinds: scripted patrols, or very simple (non-AI) attack-the-player and defend-the-buildings behaviour. They are separate targets; the only pressure to engage them is that they attack the player.

### 2.7 The interface: eight icons [Manual, with Author-observed usage]
| Icon | What the manual says | Author's experience |
|---|---|---|
| Robot ID | Identifies enemies detected on the short-range map | "Totally pointless", never used |
| Scanner | Scans the immediate vicinity for objects on or just under the ground; shows power pods or raw material (triangle) and a piece of the supreme robot (circle); anything shown is picked up automatically | Range effectively zero (only the current pixel); used after destroying a building to find the drop. Regarded as a bug |
| Status | Shows power pods, missiles, guns, damage, and the number of pieces collected | Normally not needed, theoretically useful |
| Guns | Defensive: if an enemy fires a missile and the robot can counter its tracking, the guns can shoot down incoming missiles, aided by a 3D perspective overlay on the image of the missile; missiles not hit strike and damage or destroy the robot | The defence mini-game starts automatically when an inbound missile is detected, so the icon is pointless (differs from the manual) |
| Move | Trace a route on the long-range map and fire; the robot follows it if able, but may override the player's decision if it feels threatened or is in combat | Obvious and needed |
| Info | Identifies features on the long-range map | Never used; an in-game help and catalogue of enemies |
| Radio | Two requests (see 2.9) | Used to call in support |
| Missiles | Short-range offence (see 2.8) | Attack enemies |
- The map has two modes: a long-range map (not magnified, from the robot's computer banks and intelligence reports) and a short-range map (magnified, own position at the centre, from the short-range detector). [Manual]
- Windows pop up over a persistent dashboard. [Author-observed screenshot] The dashboard is a framed grid of icon panels, a name bar for the current robot form, and the pop-up drawn over the middle.
- Controls: direction keys or joystick, fire, deselect, **hold** (freezes the game), abandon, release, and **save** (to tape; not possible while the robot feels threatened). [Manual]

### 2.8 The missile mini-game [Manual, Author-observed]
- Targets can be enemies, power plants, mines, beacons or city domes, chosen by placing a cross on the short-range map. Only targets within the missile's range can be chosen. [Manual, Author-observed] There is **no fuel mechanic** on the Amstrad. [Author-observed]
- The missile carries a camera. The player must guide it through hills and mountain ranges to the target, with very quick reactions needed. [Manual]
- **View** [Author-observed]: forward-looking wireframe: black background, yellow frame, red horizon line, blue jagged mountain silhouettes, and a yellow marker at the top showing the direction of the target (it starts centred).
- **Controls**: left and right only. No speed or altitude control; the missile flies straight and level. Mountains are the only real obstacle; all the mountains are identical and randomly placed.
- **Terminal phase**: close to the target, a line-art image of the target appears and the player must fly into it.
- **Duration**: about 30 seconds, varying with distance but not linearly.
- **Failure**: hitting a mountain explodes the missile (mushroom-cloud animation and sound) and returns the player to the dashboard. Failing to fly into the target gives a "missed" popup. Either way the player spends the seconds and one missile from a limited stock, and can fire again.
- Only the targeted object exists in the flight view, even if other enemies or a building stand behind it.
- When a building is destroyed, the item it drops lands on the exact pixel the missile hit, even though the building is much bigger, so the player must remember exactly where they fired. [Author-observed; the main tedium]

### 2.9 The radio [Manual, Author-observed]
1. **Long-range missile strike**: choose a power plant, mine, beacon or dome on the long-range map. The missile homes in on its own and takes about 30 seconds to arrive. [Manual]
2. **Spacecraft pick-up**: dispatches a spacecraft to collect a piece found in the field, which upgrades the player's robot. [Manual]
- In practice the strike is used on the power plant to open the shield window; any missile attack on the plant triggers the deflector, so the player's own missile could also open it. The strike is the hands-off version. [Manual, Author-observed]
- Call-ins are unlimited, only a cooldown applies. [Author-observed]
- The author sees this as an awkward mechanic that probably tried to add skill and timing: if a strike can be called in, why are the player's own missiles needed? The shield window (about 45 seconds) against a flight (about 30 seconds) allows one shot, or two if the target is close.

### 2.9a The missile-defence mini-game [Author-observed, from two screenshots]
- It **starts automatically** when incoming missiles are detected. There is no real in-flight "incoming missile" with a time of flight; the attack instantly starts the mini-game. (The manual describes selecting the Guns icon; the Amstrad version does not need it.)
- View: a tunnel drawn as two blue walls (the author's reading: an abstract representation of orientation, not a real thing). Incoming missiles are drawn as pyramid-like wireframes flying towards the player; they grow as they approach (far: a small triangle near the centre; close: several large overlapping pyramids).
- The player fires guns to destroy the incoming missiles. Outbound fire is shown as yellow stars.
- Closer reading of the screenshots: the "tunnel" is not a full tube but two blue trapezoid walls, one each side, angled in toward a central gap (a bow-tie shape). The yellow stars appear in pairs, one each side, on the inner edges of the walls, with smaller clusters further in toward the centre. This suggests two guns firing inward from the sides, their streams converging into depth. [Inferred from screenshots]
- There is no crosshair, ship or ground in the view: it is purely the robot's point of view.
- Aiming is up, down, left and right; the walls move as the player aims, to give some perspective. [Author-observed]
- A missile that is not destroyed hits the robot and damages or destroys it. [Manual]
- Which enemies trigger it: those with missiles (the manual lists missile systems on haulers, defenders, hunters and the bosses; patrollers have guns only).

### 2.10 Energy [Manual, Author-observed]
- The status panel lists power pods, and the scanner picks up power pods dropped by haulers. The batteries at the bottom of the dashboard are probably these pods. [Author-observed] There is only **one bank**, drawn left to right across the bottom; the apparent two banks in the screenshot are just an awkward layout, with the rightmost cells used first. It ticks down slowly. The author's reading is that energy is a mild pressure to stop the player standing around without making progress. What drains it, and what happens at empty, is left open for now.

### 2.11 Reception [Reviews]
Very high scores (for example 96% in one Spectrum review). Praise: depth, the windowing and icon system, the missile sequence, and simple individual mini-games that interlock. Criticism: a steep first hour (hard to recover even the first piece), weak sound on Spectrum, wobbly scrolling on C64.

## 3. Author-direction (what the author has said they want)

1. Start with a small proof of concept (a couple of buildings, minimal enemies) and build up to a full map.
2. The missile mini-game is the core and the most memorable part; the drive of the game is exploring and the excitement of finding an upgrade.
3. **Design principle:** the missile flight is a mini-game inside the exploration game. It must not become a flight simulator with another game as a wrapper; balance "abstract guidance of a cruise missile" against flight-sim detail.
4. The finding is not the challenge in the flight (the marker gives the direction); the fun is the dodge, then the hit as the dopamine payoff.
5. Fix the drop-location annoyance (item lands on the exact pixel hit).
6. Fix the scanner (range of zero is a bug); redesign it at least a little.
7. Add variety to the mountains and terrain in the missile flight.
8. Treat the radio call-in as a second weapon (for example a graphite bomb or EMP), not as fire support; no usage limit, only a cooldown; maybe an energy cost.
9. Buildings should have logical drops: mines are ore mines (so armour or energy), electronic buildings give energy or electronic parts; suggested a comms centre and a barracks in place of the beacon.
10. Escalation must not ramp up too quickly (avoid being dogpiled).
11. Jamming and other countermeasures should only appear on later, more powerful enemies; a standard enemy is just a standard enemy.
12. The missile is a cruise missile: it never turns a full circle, so the direction marker does not wrap; in fiction it is launched vertically and settles into flight toward the target.
13. The enemy attack and missile-defence mini-game was parked for later; it has since been discussed (sections 2.9a and 4.8).
14. Controls: arrow keys, or possibly the mouse. The original used a joystick, which is rare on Windows laptops.
15. Inbound attacks cannot occur during an outbound missile flight. There is no in-game reason and the design should not try to handwave one; it simply does not happen.
16. Attack length and damage per missile depend on the enemy. A standard enemy is about 10 seconds with fairly low damage. No attack should last more than about a minute, because longer gets boring or frustrating. A damage model has not been discussed in full yet.
17. Zero shields is death. Without a real failure state it is not a game but a walking simulator. **As built (#41):** the robot is destroyed the moment its last plate breaks, mid-attack; a "ROBOT DESTROYED" screen shows the wreck for 2 seconds, then Space starts a new game from the beginning (decided, author: there are no saves yet; a save select may come once saving exists, item 19).
18. Zero energy is also death, or at least immobilisation (death unless energy can be picked up nearby). **Decided (author, #15): immediate death**, and each death must tell the player why it happened. **As built:** energy drains slowly with running time and faster while walking; at or below two cells a "LOW POWER" warning flashes over the gauge. At zero the screen the robot was on freezes, flickers, fades to black and collapses to a line as the power fails, with a power-down sound and no explosion; only then does a quiet "POWER EXHAUSTED, ALL SYSTEMS OFFLINE" appear, then the prompt to start again. A shield death keeps its explosion and now reads "SHIELDS DOWN, HULL BREACHED" under "ROBOT DESTROYED".
19. Saving (agreed): death means reloading the last save. The player can save at any time the robot is not threatened (as in the original), plus an autosave whenever a piece is collected, so a death never costs more than the stretch since the last upgrade. No saving while threatened.
20. Shields recharge while the robot stands still and is not threatened, paid for in energy. Only damaged plates recharge; lost plates need an armour pickup (agreed, section 4.9). **As built (#82):** see the recharge bullet in section 4.9.
21. Engagement range can scale with enemy level: harder enemies may have longer range or open fire earlier.
22. Enemy aggression is tunable and should fit the role: a hauler is predominantly defensive, a patrol does its job and investigates (section 4.10).
23. Tougher enemies and perhaps military installations could have point defences against the player's missile, mirroring the player's own defensive gun. Preferred form: a hazard in the missile's terminal phase. No dashboard counter for now (section 4.11).

## 4. Proposals (from the discussion, not yet confirmed)

### 4.1 Missile flight
**Principles and tests for any new control** (derived from the author's principle above):
1. Does it create a decision (such as a route choice), or just more hand-work? If removing it would not change what the player chooses, drop it.
2. Can the whole state be read at a glance, with no instruments to learn?
3. Does it fit within roughly 30 seconds, with no ramp-up or settling in?
4. Does it raise the skill ceiling without raising the floor?

**Pacing:** keep tuning flight time so a shot is always worth the tension regardless of distance.

**Heading model:** the missile launches already aimed at the target, so the marker starts centred (confirmed on the Amstrad). It only moves off-centre when the player steers to dodge, and it shows how far off course the player is. When far off course it clamps to the edge of the strip. Optionally cap lateral drift, which also gives terrain a natural width. Steering inertia would fit naturally. A short vertical launch and pitch-over could play before the player gets control.

**Control variants to prototype against the classic baseline (left and right only):**
- Altitude: if tried, prefer discrete bands (low and high) over continuous altitude, so mountains have heights and routes become a choice.
- Throttle: speed against reaction time.
- Steering inertia.
- Interference from enemy countermeasures.
- Open question: was the fun in the dodging or in the finding? The author's answer so far: the dodge, then the hit.

**Distance indicator:** undecided whether it adds or removes fun. If tried, keep it implicit (marker grows, brightens or pulses, or a sonar-style ping that speeds up) and prototype it as a toggle (none, marker change, audio ping).

**Polish where the payoff is:** target line art filling the screen, explosion, building collapse, drop. Keep the miss low-key; consider distinguishing a near miss from a wide one.

**Terrain variety:**
- Shape types that ask different questions: narrow spikes, wide ridges, gaps and passes, and mixed density for a rhythm of tense and calm stretches.
- The visible silhouette is the hitbox, so what the player sees is exactly what kills them.
- Fairness: generated terrain must always leave a passable path.
- Readability: avoid the tangle of overlapping lines seen in dense clusters in the original; use depth fading or line-weight variation.
- Region themes (ice, desert, canyon) cosmetic first, mechanical later.
- Let the terrain between launcher and target shape the flight (hills in the way give denser mountains), which makes terrain matter and links the map to the mini-game. Terrain difficulty could also scale with stronghold depth.

**Radar and countermeasures (later):**
- Evolve the direction marker into a single radar-style element on the top edge: position shows bearing, size or pulse shows range; interference effects sit on the same element. Do not add a second display.
- Interference degrades information and never removes it: wobbling or lagging bearing, short dropouts, false contacts.
- The missile has no compass, so a dropout needs a "ghost marker": it freezes in a dimmer colour and the game moves it as the player steers, with error building up over time.
- Keep dropouts short; the terminal phase appears at close range regardless of radar state.
- **As built: jamming removes the marker, briefly (author: "full removal for a short time, flickering etc to give the effect of jamming. Perhaps our view also shows interference?").** *Overrides "degrades and never removes" and the ghost marker above.* A jammed target's flight has short spells, drawn from the flight's seed, when the direction marker is gone: it flickers out as a spell starts, is absent through it, and flickers back as it ends, with SIGNAL LOST where it was. The view breaks up with it: torn bands of the picture slip sideways, static, streaks and a rolling band, all stronger as the jamming deepens. The picture is broken up, never blanked, because terrain can still kill. Steering is untouched: jamming takes information away, never control. Calm for the first 3 seconds, spells of 1.2 to 2.2 seconds with 2.5 to 4.5 seconds between, and every spell over a second before the terminal phase, so the target is never jammed once in sight (placeholders, `config.jammer`). A wash of static sounds as each spell begins. Which targets jam is for escalation to decide; until then a feel-test toggle (`jammingEverywhere`) jams every target. A target can carry both jamming and point defence: hard at both ends of the flight, still one hit to kill.
- Countermeasures (jammers, chaff and so on) only on later enemies, so the early game stays as simple as the original. Enemy countermeasures are something the player flies through (a wobbling marker, decoys), with no dashboard step. **Deferred by the author:** player jamming as a pre-launch counter on the dashboard. The author is uncertain about jamming a jammer at all, as it sounds like an arms race.
- Prototype: keep the simple marker and make the bearing source swappable.

**One target per flight:** keep the flight abstract. Fiction fix: the missile is a seeker locked to one designated signature, and a miss means overshoot and loss of lock. Rejected for now: showing several objects in the terminal phase (readability, tuning cost, flight-sim creep). Optional later: splash damage, and near-miss feedback.

### 4.2 Scanner redesign
Manual and author agree on how the original works: the scanner is used after destroying a dome, standing in the ruins, and anything it shows is picked up automatically.

**Decided [Author]:** nothing about a building's contents can be found before it is destroyed, so the unknown contents are part of the loot-box pull. Drops and parts are only findable after the building is gone, lying in its ruin.

**Decided [Author]: sharp blips on the mini map.** A drop always lies on its ruin's cell, and the player knows where their ruins are, so there is no location to work out and fuzzy, triangulated blips have nothing to do. Instead, each scan marks every ruin within the ring's reach that still holds an uncollected drop. A mark lights as the ring passes over it and fades after a few seconds (placeholder in config), on both the local and the full map, so the player remembers or scans again. This replaces the bearing and close/far readout, which pointed at a place the player could already see; the readout now just counts what the scan found. On screen, drops are called **salvage** (decided, author), in keeping with the investigator's field labels (section 4.13): the readout says "SALVAGE AT 2 SITES" and "SALVAGE RECOVERED 1". "Drop" stays as the design and code term. Scan's job is "which of my ruins are worth walking to", paid for by the chance of waking hunters (section 4.10). If that proves too thin in play, the passive option below (nearby ruins show their contents without a verb) is the fallback.

The rest of this section is the earlier proposal, which assumed some pre-fire scanning. It is kept for the record; where it conflicts with the decisions above, they win.

The proposal gives it a different job:
- A ping with a radius, shown on the map, with the radius as an upgrade stat.
- Graded information, with fixed ranges (not tied to the size of a town, so the experience is the same everywhere): within 1 cell the drop is picked up; within 6 cells a blip shows with no direction; within 12 cells the direction shows. The numbers are placeholders in config. The player already knows roughly where to scan, since a drop lands where the missile hit, so the scan is a search around that spot.
- A pacing cost: a cooldown or a small drain on the batteries.
- Pickup by proximity once drops land at the building's centre.
- Role: with about 80 domes and six pieces, blind-firing is hopeless. Scan should tell the player which buildings are worth a missile (a shot at an empty building is wasted), which also removes the "each town has one thing" predictability.
- Risk: scan could make finding upgrades too easy. Keep it a puzzle with **triangulation** (signal strength grows as you get closer to the building with the upgrade, so scanning from two or three spots narrows it down), or a partial reveal (a cluster or area, with only close range pinning down the building).

- **Question from the author (not a request): replace Scan with a constantly scanning radar screen? How would that differ from the mini map?** Answer: it would not differ, so no separate radar screen; two always-on top-down views of the same space are redundant, and the mini map already shows enemies in time for first strike. The real choice is passive versus active sensing of building contents, with the result shown as a layer on the mini map either way:
  - Passive: contents of nearby buildings appear once within sensor range (extended by upgrades); no Scan verb; exploration becomes a routing question (getting close costs energy and exposes the player to defenders). Simpler; risk of a drive-past-every-dome checklist.
  - Active: the ping with an energy or cooldown cost and triangulation. Keeps a small puzzle and a spending decision; risk of spam if cheap.
  - Lean: keep the active ping for now (the only detective work in the game, and tied to the energy economy), but carry both in the PoC behind a toggle. If players just hammer the ping, switch to passive and drop the Scan verb.
- **Author's idea for the active form: the Scan button adds a radar pulse to the mini map** (a green sweeping arm, or a concentric ring expanding outward) that shows blips for items. Recommended form: the expanding ring. One press is one pulse (a sweeping arm reads as an always-on radar); the ring travels out and stops at the sensor limit, showing range without a number, and upgrades visibly extend it; blips light up as the ring passes. Refinements: blips are fuzzy at a distance (a soft blob covering a few buildings) and sharpen up close (triangulation without numbers); blips fade after a few seconds, so the player remembers or pulses again, and each pulse costs energy.

- **Author's idea: going active on radar gives the player away** (the game is not really a stealth game), waking up any enemies that pick up the spike. Proposal:
  - Model: a pulse is a noise event at the player's position with the ring's radius. Only enemies inside the ring react. No visibility meters or sneaking.
  - Reactions follow the role stances (section 4.10): a patroller comes to investigate; a hauler moves away (pinging near one scares off the energy supply); a defender goes on alert if its dome is inside the ring; a hunter heads for the source.
  - Scan becomes risk against reward: information about buildings in exchange for some of the first-strike advantage. This answers the "click me" worry better than an energy price.
  - Exposure can replace the energy cost: keep a short cooldown, charge little or no energy, so fewer things drain the batteries.
  - Deliberate use: enemies that come to investigate arrive on the mini map on the player's terms, so pinging to draw a patroller in and striking first is a legitimate tactic (as with the beacon as a boss lure).
  - Anti-snowball: the response is "come and look", not "attack at once"; the cap on simultaneous attackers still applies.

### 4.3 Power plant, shield window and the second weapon
- The original power plant cannot be destroyed (it survives by switching to a deflector shield), so the open question "can a missile destroy it?" is answered: **no**. Keep it indestructible; this keeps the window mechanic clean.
- Reframe the radio strike as a **utility weapon that disables power** (for example a graphite bomb, which shorts out a grid with conductive filaments, or an EMP) and not as firepower. Only the player's missiles damage anything. Different interaction from the missile: no flight. (First proposed as picking a spot on the long-range map; as built, the player chooses a power station with the cross on the local map, the same way as a missile target, so it is always clear which network is being cut. Only stations inside the local view can be chosen, section 4.6.)
- **Missiles on shielded domes (author's decision):** the missile is used up and does not kill the target. Firing again is not blocked. Without the power being disabled first, the next shot fails too, so the cost is a missile, not a lockout.
- **Suppression is checked at launch, not at impact (decided).** A missile launched while the network is suppressed is locked to that target, so it destroys the building even if the window closes during its flight. A shot launched after the window closes is checked against the shields as normal. This keeps "shields back online during the first shot" from happening.
- **A crash into terrain does not touch the window (decided).** Only the window timer ends suppression, so a missile that hits a mountain leaves the window open for another shot. A missile that reaches a building does not end the window either.
- **The shield window is a real-time timer that keeps running during an attack (decided).** The launch-time check means it never needs to pause. The old "one shot, or two if close" line is replaced: the number of shots is limited by the window length and flight time, not by a fixed count.
- **Shield visuals (agreed).** A powered building shows a faint shield ring in the terminal phase, so the shield is readable before the missile lands. On impact the ring flashes and the blast stops at it, the building survives, and the screen shows "DEFLECTED" instead of "HIT".
- **Window length is a guess (author).** The 45-second figure in section 2 was the author's guess, not taken from the original. Treat it as a placeholder in config until play-tested.
- **Timers keep running during an engagement (author).** The author's view is that timers run, which I read as covering cooldowns as well as the window. Confirm if cooldowns should pause instead.
- **Grid Disable costs no energy, only a cooldown (author).** Energy cost is not used for now; it can be added later if the verb feels too free in play. On screen the cooldown reads as **CHARGING** with the seconds left (decided, author): "cooldown" is a game-design word and breaks the fourth wall. "Cooldown" stays as the design and code term.
- **The EMP verb is called Grid Disable for now (author).** Renaming is cheap, so the name can change later. It is backlog issue #13.
- **All buildings are shielded, not only domes (author's decision).** This departs from the original, where only domes had a force field. Each network has one power station, and it powers every building shield in that network. Opening the shield window knocks out the power, so every building in the network is exposed at once. Shields are on buildings only for now; the robot and enemies have none.
- **Alert side-effect (decided, author): the hunter patrols the domes.** Cutting power is detected at the power station, not at the robot: the network knows something is wrong but not where. So the network's hunter leaves its post and makes rounds of the network's intact domes, nearest first, then each of the others, then again, until the power comes back; then it returns to its post. With no dome left it waits beside the power station. If it sees the robot at any point (hunters are always looking, section 4.10) it is alerted and the normal chase and attack pacing take over. Only the cut network's hunter responds; an alerted or destroyed hunter does not; cutting again while the power is down only extends the rounds. Why: the payoff of cutting the power is the shield window, so the outage should cost something, not pay twice (author). The first version sent the hunter to search the station, which let the player cut a far station to pull the guard away: shields down and the guard gone, at no cost. Now the guard walks among exactly the buildings the player wants to hit, so the window is a timing puzzle (watch the rounds, strike while it is on the far side) and is read from behaviour: the player sees it leave its post and start its rounds. Accepted consequence: "kill the guard first, then cut" becomes the usual order again; it is the garrisons (section 4.4) that keep a town with no guard pushing back, not the outage. Open: whether an outage should also stir the garrisons in the cut network.
- PoC: cooldown only (as in the original) plus the alert side-effect above. Add energy only if it feels too free in play, and only as a general resource shared by several systems. Upgrades could extend the window, add charges or widen the area. Later enemies could have hardened power plants.
- **Piece collection is automatic, not a verb** (author's decision). The original radio's second function was calling a spacecraft to collect a piece; since it would always be used, it carries no decision. When the player reaches a piece, the pick-up happens by itself with a short upgrade sequence (the craft arrives, the robot grows, cells and plates extend), followed by the autosave. Optional, to try in play: the pick-up waits until the robot is not threatened, so a piece found mid-fight means dealing with the fight first; drop it if it only feels like a delay.
- A building protected by shields means using a window on the comms centre (if added) means not using it on a dome that might hold a piece: a real trade-off with no special rules.

### 4.4 Building types and drops
The original already has four types (dome, power plant, mine, beacon), each with its own icon and line art. What is undefined is the drops. Proposal, following the author's logic:
| Building | Drops (proposal) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Dome | Mostly nothing; occasionally supplies; the six pieces | The haystack |
| Power plant | None (indestructible) | Shield switch; energy comes from hauler cargo instead |
| Mine | Armour or repair material, or energy | Ore |
| Comms centre (replaces beacon) | Electronics: minor boosts | Alerts the two bosses (as the original beacon); who builds a "beacon"? |
| Barracks (author's suggestion) | Missiles and ammo | Not a garrison (decided, author: see below). Parked as a possible missile-restock depot for the economy (#15) |
| Haulers (enemy) | Cargo: power pods and raw material (as the original) | Gives energy resupply without a destructible power plant |
- Electronic parts: treat as direct minor boosts (a little scan radius, some resistance to enemy countermeasures), not a parts currency, unless the PoC shows one is needed.
- *Superseded by "Supplies and parts" below.* As built (#15): a drop was energy or armour, one per building at most: a comms centre always gave energy, a mine or a dome either, fixed per building, at a flat 50% chance. Domes then held most of the world's supplies, and the building type made little difference to what a ruin held.
- **Supplies and parts (decided, author).** What a ruin holds comes in two kinds, handled differently:
  - **Supplies are rolled.** Each building kind has its own chance for each supply, rolled independently when the world is made, so a ruin can hold nothing, one supply or several. The kind shapes what is likely without making anything certain, which keeps the unknown contents as the pull (section 4.2). Placeholder chances (config), energy / armour: dome 15% / 15%, mine 30% / 60%, comms centre 70% / 10%. About seven domes in ten hold nothing, so domes are the haystack without a special rule; a mine leans to armour (ore) and a comms centre to energy (electronics), so a player short of one has a reason to pick a building. Each resource can be tuned on its own. The power station and the enemies hold nothing for now; enemy wrecks can join the same table later (for example missiles and armour from an armed enemy). Each supply is collected on its own: energy restores two cells, armour fully mends the two weakest plates, broken ones first (section 4.9), and one that would be wasted (its gauge full) stays in the ruin while the others are taken. A scan marks a ruin while it holds anything uncollected.
  - **Per-unit rolls, and enemy wrecks (decided, author).** *Refines the bullet above.* Each kind lists, for each supply, a maximum number of units and one chance, and every unit up to the maximum is rolled on its own at that chance. A unit is one cell, one plate fully mended (the weakest, broken first) or one missile. Why: a single roll for a bundle ("50% for two missiles") is all or nothing; one chance per unit gives the same average with a smoother spread, from the same two numbers. Considered and rejected as too complex: a separate chance for each unit. Buildings keep their averages: what was a p% chance of two is now two units at p% each. Pickup takes each unit on its own, so a wreck with two missiles and a rack with room for one gives one and keeps the other. A side effect: buildings are less often completely empty, since each of several units can turn up (at the old dome chances, 18 of 40 domes in the default world held nothing, against 28 before; with domes as the arsenal, below, none do). **Wrecks** are searched like ruins, where the enemy fell, not at its post, and hold what the machine is built for (placeholders): a **hunter**, the aggressive piece, carries ammunition and burns energy on the chase: up to 2 missiles at 60% each, 2 cells at 40% each, 1 plate at 25%. A **defender**, the defensive piece, is built heavy: up to 2 plates at 55% each, 1 missile at 40%, 1 cell at 20%. So a player short of missiles hunts, and one short of armour opens garrisoned domes. A **patroller** holds nothing: it carries guns, not missiles, and it is killed to stop it fetching help (section 4.10), not for loot; loot would make killing every patroller automatic. If patrollers end up ignored, the fix is their behaviour, not their loot. A kill never pays for itself on average: about 1.3 missiles per kill with misses, against 1.2 back from a hunter and 0.4 from a defender. Attacks are unchanged: hunters and defenders still share the same attack. **Domes are the arsenal (decided, author: "it's a shooty game, let people shoot").** Enemies rearm at domes, so domes hold missiles: up to 3 at 60% each (placeholder), 1.8 on average against about 1.33 spent per target with misses. The default world then holds about 100 missiles, about what it takes to destroy everything, so the rack paces the player between domes rather than starving them. Why: a rough count with domes at 2 at 20% gave a net loss of about 0.8 missiles per target opened, so a full rack lasted about ten targets, while finding six blind-placed parts means opening most of the 45 candidate buildings: unwinnable. Considered: clues to parts before destruction (changes the rule that nothing is known before), refunds for deflected missiles, and fewer candidate buildings; none closed the gap alone. Consequence: domes are rarely empty, so the haystack is now about parts, not supplies. **Energy supply raised (decided, author).** A rough count of a playthrough: the six parts lie in 45 candidate buildings, so the last is found after opening about 39 of them on average, or about 60 targets with the enemies met on the way. The clock drains during missile flights too, so that is about 47 cells or more (about 27 for 80 flights of about 30 seconds, 10 for recharge, 7 for walking, 3 for attacks, plus searching) against 44 in all, so an average player ran dry before the sixth part. Raised to about 59 (placeholders): domes 2 cells at 25% each (were 15%), comms centres 3 at 70% (were 2), hunters 2 at 50% (were 40%). Domes get the most because that is where the time goes; comms centres stay the place to go when low. Considered: pausing the drain during flights (the clock is meant to press against idling, and a flight is not idling) and slowing the drain; kept the drain as it is so shooting still costs time. If play shows players avoiding fights to save energy, pausing the drain during flights is the cleaner fix. These are averages from rough per-target timings; the first-strike rate and the real time per target are the unknowns to measure in play. **Every dome holds at least one missile (decided, author):** one guaranteed, then 2 more at 40% each, so the average stays 1.8 and only the zero goes. At 3 at 60%, about one dome in sixteen held no missile, and firing the last missile at one left the rack empty on the dice alone. Now a hit on a dome always pays its missile back. This refines the no-floor decision rather than reversing it: a miss, a crash or a shield deflection with the last missile still empties the rack, so running dry comes from the player's shots, not bad luck. Side effect: no dome is ever empty, so a scan marks every opened dome until its missile is taken, and a blip says less about parts; parts may need a blip of their own.
  - **Parts are placed, not rolled.** Parts are a fixed, limited set, distributed over the world by a separate placement pass, not a dice roll per building or wreck. So the set is always complete, nothing is duplicated, and progress can be shown ("3 of 6"). **As built:** six parts (placeholder), at most one per building (a part can share a ruin with supplies); at most two in any network, so some networks hold none and a few hold two; none in the starting town; only in a building whose ruin can be walked to from the starting town with every other building still standing; chosen at random, weighted three to one for domes over comms centres (mines and the power station hold none). Each part has an identity (0 to 5), ready for upgrades to give each its own effect. A part is collected automatically on reaching its ruin, never left behind as a wasted supply can be; a scan marks its ruin like a supply's; the scan readout shows "PARTS 2/6". For now a part is only a collectable: the upgrade sequence, autosave and what each part does come with upgrades. Later, placement becomes dependency-aware (section 5).
  - **Missiles (as built, #86).** The robot carries a rack of 8 (placeholder), full at the start, shown as a third gauge of upright orange rounds under the energy and shields. Every launch uses one, whether it hits, misses, crashes or is deflected by a shield; grid disable costs none. Missiles come only from ruins, as a third supply column: dome 20%, mine 30%, comms centre 10% (placeholders), each restoring 2, never past a full rack; while the rack is full a missile supply stays in its ruin, like the others. With an empty rack the Missile verb reads RACK EMPTY and the targeting cross does not open. **No floor (decided, author):** an empty rack is bad luck. The player can still walk to uncollected ruins, and if none are left the energy clock ends the game. Considered and rejected: slow fabrication from energy, and a last missile that refills itself over time. Rack size budget: clearing a whole town costs about 10 missiles with misses, the worthwhile targets about 5 or 6, so 8 forces a choice of which domes are worth opening. Later, upgrades can add slots.
  - Considered and rejected: rearming at an intact enemy dome during a power cut. The robot is alone in hostile country, and calling in at an enemy base to restock does not fit (author).
- Missile restock: unknown in the original (see open questions). The barracks or hauler cargo are candidates.
- Optional later: a radar building that detects the player and feeds the comms centre; the grid-disable could silence comms during the window.
- **Garrisons: defenders live in the domes, at random (decided, author; issue #62).** Each network has from 0 to 4 defenders (the number drawn per network), each assigned at random to one of its domes, at most one per dome. Hitting a dome wakes its defender if it has one: a missile that destroys the dome brings it out of the rubble, one the shield deflects brings it out of the door. Only the hit dome's defender wakes, not its neighbours: not knowing which domes are occupied is the uncertainty. Why: with one guard per town and nothing else, a town is "kill the guard, then cut power, open a dome, scan, repeat"; a garrison makes every dome opened a possible fight, so the player chooses which domes are worth it, and the cost comes in small pieces rather than all at once. Considered and rejected: one barracks per town holding the garrison. It is a single decisive building: either worth destroying (and then destroying it sets the whole garrison on the player) or holding only one or two defenders and not worth anything. Dome count: towns need at least four domes for four defenders to fit; if a network draws more defenders than it has domes, the extra are not placed. Budget check (section 4.9): a woken defender costs about half an average attack, since choosing Missile stops time and a sharp player often strikes first; at up to 4 per network (2 on average), flattening a whole town costs the guard plus about one attack, so armour drops may need to be more generous, or n lowered, after play. Later, n may rise with distance from the start network. Scan does not wake defenders for now (section 4.2 planned that it would): with the garrison hidden at random it would make every scan near a town a gamble on top of the hunter roll.

### 4.5 Escalation and pacing
- The original is gentle: the beacon only alerts the two bosses, and enemy replacement is limited by the dome supply chain (power and raw material). PoC: start with exactly this.
- Idea: because the alert is a boss-finder, the player can use it deliberately: a risk early, a lure to trigger when ready in the endgame.
- If more is added (a barracks spawning defenders, reinforcement calls), apply these guardrails:
  - Finite garrisons: a limited pool and slow spawn rate; destroying the source stops spawns.
  - Hard cap on active attackers (about 2-3 at once), extras queue; important for the missile-defence mini-game.
  - Reinforcements take time to arrive along the passes, so they can be seen coming.
  - A visible alert dial with stages (calm, suspicious, alarmed, full alert); bosses at the top stage.
  - Recovery: the alert decays if the player leaves or goes quiet; it stays local to a network.
  - Comms cooldown: one call per period.
  - "Dogpile check" in the PoC: log the maximum number of simultaneous attackers and the time from alert to first arrival.
- Principle: escalation should be pressure the player can read and manage, not a trap they cannot recover from.
- **Agreed: difficulty by distance from spawn (author).** Tougher enemy types are placed at generation in networks further from where the player starts; nothing is upgraded in place (author: "upgrading existing enemies doesn't make sense").
  - The curve follows the route: near towns are soft, far towns tough, and the player works outward collecting upgrades (4.4), so upgrades stay a gain with no reason to hold back.
  - Distance is path distance through the passes, not straight-line, as parts placement already measures reachability.
  - A gradient, not a hard edge: for example, near networks standard types, middle networks some carrying point defence (4.11), far networks point defence and jamming (4.1). Banding to be proposed.
  - Some parts are biased toward far networks, so the player goes there with their best upgrades.
  - A tougher type has its own look on the map and in the flight art (#112), so the player sees a defended region before committing. Tougher means harder to reach, never more health: one hit still kills.
  - Replacement (#70) is world simulation, not a difficulty tool: a replacement is the same type as what it replaces.
- **As built:** towns are ranked by walking distance from the start town over the terrain; the nearest 40% are near (always including the start town), the next 35% middle, the rest far. In the middle, each hunter and defender is hardened at 50%; in the far towns, all are. A hardened hunter jams (author's choice); a hardened defender carries point defence, and in the far towns half also jam. Patrollers roam between towns and stay standard. Hardened machines are more likely to carry missiles (author: "we need to use more"): a hunter up to 3 at 75% each, a defender up to 2 at 70%. At least 2 of the 6 parts lie in far towns. Placeholders in `config.localMap`. **Their look (#112):** a point-defence machine carries a turret with twin barrels, a jamming one a mast with signal arcs, drawn in the warning (tracer) colour on top of its art in the flight; on the map, where the fittings are too small to read alone, the whole icon is in that colour. Targeting one shows GUNS and/or JAMS beside IN RANGE.
- **Considered and set aside: escalation by damage done.** A hidden, one-way stage raised by material lost, hardening machines in place or only through replacements. Set aside because upgrading existing machines does not make sense, and replacements would rarely be built: the player destroys a town's domes, its factories, early, as they are the arsenal. Damage may still be kept as one light trigger, such as the bosses coming out (#71); undecided.

### 4.6 Dashboard and verbs
- Verb set: **Move, Scan, Missile and Grid-disable**. Extract was dropped (author: any opportunity to use it would always be taken, so it is just a button labelled "click me"); collection is automatic (section 4.3). Status is folded into an ambient display (batteries, damage, the state-of-mind trace). Cut the robot ID icon and the info icon (replace with contextual information on selected targets, and optionally a catalogue that fills in as enemies are met). Guns become a reactive mini-game, not an icon.
- Energy and shields display: the original swapped one or the other into the same bar across the bottom of the dashboard (author). With modern resolution, show both at once (author). **Agreed by the author:** segmented cells for each, battery cells for energy and armour plates for shields, rather than plain bars. Reasons: segments are tunable units (a standard missile costs one plate, a cargo pickup restores two cells); hits and pickups are visible events; upgrades visibly add cells and plates; it fits the robot projecting its own state. Use different shapes as well as colours so they are never confused. Energy drains gradually within the current cell; shields drop in whole plates. Low state: flash the last cell or plate and agitate the state-of-mind trace.
- Segmented battery cells are glanceable and diegetic. If energy stays as a pacing clock, define what happens when it runs low (reduced range, slower recovery, shakier systems) and not a sudden end.
- Use our own dashboard arrangement, icon art and branding; the panel-grid-with-overlapping-windows idea is fine, the specific layout, icons and logo are the original's expression.
- **The mini map's two zooms have different jobs (decided, author).** The **full map** is for planning and travel: it marks each network by its power station (#90; it used to be an oval at the town's extent, which hid the terrain), drawn in the shield colour inside a dashed ring while its power is cut, and the salvage blips from scans, but no other buildings and no enemies. Only high ground is drawn on it; ground and valleys are left blank, since at a few pixels a cell they were noise. The town area still decides which network the robot is in; only the drawing changed. The **local map** is where the robot's equipment works: Missile and Grid Disable can only target what the local view shows, and choosing Missile, Grid Disable or Scan from the full map switches back to local. The equipment's reach is the local view's rectangle (12 cells left and right, 5 up and down), not a radius. This is not realistic, but it feels right in the game (author): what the player can see is what they can hit. The targeting cross is clamped to the view, so it cannot be moved off the mini map. Move works on either zoom; on the full map its cross covers the whole world, for long trips. Open, to try in play: on the full map the robot walks blind, so it may need to switch back to local when a hunter comes into view or is alerted, and the cross may need bigger steps across the whole world.

### 4.7 Map and generation
- Concerns [Author-direction]: every area must be reachable and the game winnable; drops must be spaced to reward exploring everywhere without a predictable "each town has one thing" pattern.
- The original already randomises which domes hold pieces each game, and only six of ten networks hold any.
- Reachability: generate then validate on the graph; hybrid option: hand-designed network layouts placed on a generated graph (a network is a blob of eight domes plus the support buildings).
- **No links between networks (decided, author; issue #21).** What matters is that each network is a self-contained town, recognisable as one, and that every network can be walked to, or the game cannot be finished. The original's graph of passes is not modelled: the map is one terrain grid with the towns placed on it. Reachability is checked on that terrain, not on a graph: from the robot's real start, with power stations counted as walls (they can never be destroyed) and other buildings not (they can be blasted into rubble), every building must be walkable to or beside a walkable cell. The generator guarantees it (towns are connected through the fewest high-ground cells, and a building is never placed where it would cut a route), and a test checks it across many world seeds. Alerts stay local to a network (section 4.5), so nothing needs to travel between networks.
- **Town walls (decided, author; as built, #89; values are placeholders in config).** Networks were not distinctive (a town was a cleared patch with buildings), and two pairs of towns overlapped, so where they met the player could not tell which network they were in. Now: the hand-placed town positions are spread so that no town, or the ring its wall stands on, reaches another's. Every town but a fifth of them (two of ten, chosen at random) gets a ring of high ground just outside its area, out to 1.3 times its radii (more than one step of a walk can cross, so the wall has no diagonal leaks). About two thirds of the ring is wall; the rest is 1 gap (a fortress) or, with even chance, 2 (a back door, roughly opposite the first). Walls block enemies as well as the robot, so the gaps are chokepoints; sight stays distance-only, so hunters and patrollers still see, and fire, over them. Generation order: terrain, clear the towns, build the walls, join the towns (the joining pass never opens a wall cell, so routes come in through the gaps), then open a way out from every gap that does not lead out on its own, without going back through its own town. A gap is placed where it opens onto the map, not off its edge. Everything placed after (buildings, the start, hunters, patrollers, haulers) is unchanged and still checked across many seeds. Moving the towns and walling them changes the default world's layout, so which towns are near, middle and far (section 4.5), and so which machines are hardened and where parts lie, changed with it.
- Winnable progression: dependency-aware placement, so each key upgrade is somewhere reachable given what the player already has.
- Minor drops come from enemies and so spread out on their own.
- Later decision: fixed map per playthrough or seeded generation so layouts can be replayed and shared.
- **The robot starts outside the networks (decided, author).** With hunters always looking (section 4.10), starting in a town centre meant being spotted on the first frame. The robot instead starts on open ground outside every town and out of sight of every hunter, near the starting network, and decides when and how to walk in. This matches the original, where the player starts in a valley between two ranges (section 2.4).
- **Buildings and passability (decided, author).** There is no mechanic for entering buildings and none is planned. In the first builds a building filled one cell but did not block movement, so the robot could walk through a dome.
  - **Player:** intact buildings are impassable. A ruin (a building destroyed by a missile) becomes passable rubble, since any drop lies there. The power station cannot be destroyed, so it always blocks.
  - **Enemies:** can pass through buildings but never stop on a building cell. The fiction supports this: these are the enemy's own buildings (domes build and garrison robots, haulers deliver to mines and power stations), so enemies have a way in and the intruder does not. It also removes "stuck" cases: a robot built inside a dome always has a way out, and building placement cannot break an enemy route. Patrol posts, chase destinations and return points are always open ground, so an enemy the player can see is always one the player can target. Defenders on standby are literally inside their dome until alerted.
  - Readability: an enemy crossing a building should look intentional, for example a short fade or a dimmed map icon while inside.
  - If enemies cutting through buildings during a chase feels unfair in playtesting, give building cells an extra route cost for enemies so they prefer open ground. That is a tuning value, not a redesign.
  - **Passability depends on game state**, not only the terrain: the check takes the list of ruins as well as the map. The same check serves the robot's movement, the reachability preview on the map, and enemy routes (with the enemy exception above).
  - **Clicking a building** moves the robot to the nearest free cell beside it, instead of treating it as unreachable.
  - **Generation guarantee:** building placement must never cut the player's routes. Reject any placement that cuts a town off from the others, or leaves a building with no free neighbouring cell. Extend the existing "every town is reachable" test to cover this. Enemies need no such check.

### 4.8 Missile defence
- Symmetry: offence is steering a missile through obstacles, defence is shooting missiles at the player. Keep that contrast; both are short, abstract and readable at a glance.
- Automatic start is diegetic: the manual's premise is that the robot's survival drive takes over when threatened, so the reflex takes the screen. This also explains why the Guns icon is redundant. Keep it.
- Cost of the instant start: no warning and no chance to move. Add a short warning beat of one to two seconds (a sensor sound, a spike in the state-of-mind trace) so it does not feel like a random yank. Attack frequency and missiles per attack become the difficulty dials, which fits the hard cap on simultaneous attackers (section 4.5).
- Use size for distance, with a depth cue behind it (originally the tunnel walls; now the horizon and ground, see "Background" below); make the player's tracer rounds clearly distinct from the missiles.
- Stakes: a missed missile damages the robot; success costs nothing. Keep it simple unless the PoC shows it is too free.
- Interaction to decide: attacks can land during the 45-second shield window. If the window keeps running, a defence engagement costs the player shots and creates real tension; if it pauses, attacks are a free interruption. Lean: let the window run, with short engagements so an attack costs seconds, not the whole window. Feel-test.
- Decided (author): an attack cannot start during an outbound missile flight; no fiction is offered for it. Implementation note: defer an attack that comes due during a flight until the flight ends, so the rule delays attacks and does not cancel them. Missiles are limited, so chaining flights to dodge attacks is not an exploit worth worrying about.
- Controls (author): arrow keys or the mouse. Support both: the missile flight needs only left and right (arrow keys, or mouse horizontal movement), and the defence mini-game suits mouse aiming as well as arrow keys. A gamepad could come later.
- **Input between screens (decided, author).** Space fires in defence, continues after a flight result and selects on the dashboard, so a player hammering fire as an attack ended had the next presses open a verb or skip a result. Two fixes:
  - **Guard every screen change:** for about half a second after the screen changes, presses are ignored, and a key held through the change does not count until it is released and pressed again. This covers every transition, including future screens.
  - **A short attack summary:** when an attack ends, the defence view stays for about 2 seconds showing "ATTACK OVER" and the number of hits, then returns to the dashboard by itself, with no press needed. It gives the player a beat to stop firing and tells them how it went.
  - Considered: a separate fire key (Space stays as fire because it is the natural key); kept as a fallback if the guard and summary do not cure it in play.
- **Correction to the first build (author):** the first implementation played as a 2D "Space Invaders" layout: a robot at the bottom of the screen sliding left and right on lanes and firing straight up at missiles coming down. That does not match the original or the intent. Defence is a first-person turret view, not a third-person lane shooter, and must not reuse the flight's lateral steering.
- **Aiming model (decided, author): yaw and pitch within a limited arc.** Considered: Cartesian (an aim point moving over a fixed scene) and full yaw and pitch (the view turns freely). Chosen: the view turns (yaw and pitch, no roll) but is clamped to a forward arc, starting at about ±45° yaw by ±25° pitch, with every missile coming from inside that arc. Reasons:
  - Within a small arc, turning reads almost like Cartesian aiming, so the view stays readable and avoids flight-sim creep (design principle 3).
  - Missiles can start just outside the visible view, so "which one do I turn toward first" becomes a real decision without needing a full radar. The heading strip (see "Background" below) shows a missile that is off screen.
  - The background does real orientation work: it moves as the view turns.
  - **Turn rate is the dial**: it limits mouse and keys alike, so the two inputs play the same game, and it gives a natural upgrade (a faster turret).
  - The arc limits are config values, so playtesting can widen them toward full yaw and pitch or narrow them toward Cartesian without a redesign.
  - Input: arrow keys turn at the turn rate; the mouse turns the view through pointer lock (relative movement, still capped at the turn rate). Pointer lock costs a click to capture the mouse and Esc to release it.
- **View (decided, author):** our own take on the original's visual language, not a copy of it. Incoming missiles as blue wireframe pyramids that grow as they close; outbound fire as yellow stars. The aim point is the centre of the view.
- **Background (decided, author; replaces the blue walls).** The first turret build drew two blue walls flanking a central gap. They did not work: they read as solid objects rather than an orientation cue, showed poorly which way the view faced or how near the arc's edge it was, and at full turn one wall filled the middle of the view. Considered: a horizon and ground; an instruments-only view (gunsight, heading and pitch scales, a grid fixed to world bearings), which is closer to the original; a wireframe tunnel; a starfield. The tunnel and starfield were rejected. **Chosen: horizon and ground.**
  - A horizon line across the view: looking up or down moves it, turning slides the scene along it.
  - Ground below the horizon in the flight's ground colour, with sparse perspective lines: lines running toward the horizon are fixed to world bearings, so they slide as the view turns (turning feedback even with no missile in view); lines parallel to the horizon close up toward it, giving depth so a missile's size reads as distance.
  - Plain black sky, so the blue pyramids and yellow stars stand out.
  - A low, generic hill silhouette along the horizon, generated from the attack's seed. Later option: the robot's real surroundings from the map.
  - **Heading strip** along the top: shows the arc's edges, the view's heading, the launcher's bearing and a tick for each incoming missile, including those off screen. It replaces the red edge arrows.
  - Shares visual language with the missile flight (horizon, ground, hills): the flight flies forward through the terrain, defence turns in place.
- **A strike from a single enemy (decided, author).** The first turret build scattered missiles at random bearings across the arc, which felt like a swarm from everywhere rather than an attack by one enemy. Instead:
  - **One launch point.** The hunter sits at a point on the horizon and every missile of the attack starts there. **The view starts facing it**: the robot detected the inbound, so it knows where it came from, and the survival reflex turns to face the aggressor. The launcher is drawn as a simple marker for now; hunter art can replace it later.
  - **Missiles fan out from the launcher.** Each still flies a straight line in the world, from the launcher to a point near the robot. In perspective it starts as a dot at the launcher and swings outward as it closes, ending somewhere across the sky. This matches the original screenshots (small near the centre, large and spread when close), and the changing bearing means the player must lead a little. The width of the fan is a config value. This replaces the earlier fixed-bearing missiles and the separate spawn spread.
  - **Missiles come from the sky.** They leave the launcher on the horizon and come in from above, never out of the ground, so the turret's downward pitch can shrink to about 5° below the horizon.
  - **Visible and audible launches.** A muzzle flash at the launcher and a launch thump for each missile, rippling through each volley (thump, thump, thump, pause), so the player sees and hears each volley leave.
  - **Launched beyond the guns' range.** Otherwise the player could aim at the launcher and hold fire. Missiles only come into range once they have begun to fan out. The launch distance against the gun range is a config value and a strong tuning lever.
  - To discuss separately (author): how much inbound missiles vary between enemies. A single launcher is the base model; a boss might have two launchers, a wider fan or faster volleys.
- **Guns (decided, author): two guns converging on the centre.** Many games of the era (Elite, 3D Starstrike, Zoids) used the same layout. Reasons:
  - A single gun from the bottom centre runs its stream up the middle of the screen, over the aim point and the target, and brings back the rejected "cannon at the bottom" look. A single gun from the centre makes each shot a shrinking dot, so the player loses the sense of fire travelling.
  - Streams from the sides stay off the target, frame the aim point, and show depth as they close and meet.
  - It matches the screenshots' paired yellow stars.
  - Upgrade option: a second pair of guns (four in all, like a Lancaster turret) as a visible fire-rate upgrade, alongside a faster turret. Four guns are not the default: they add visual noise without gameplay on their own.
  - Parked: a convergence-range mechanic, where the streams meet at a set distance (as Second World War fighters set their guns to converge), so missiles near that range are easiest to hit. Interesting, but it adds a rule to a ten-second game; revisit only if the PoC needs more depth.
- **Shots travel (decided, author).** Stars take time to reach a missile, so the player must lead a little and choose which missile to turn toward first; this is where the "more targets than can be handled at once" pressure (section 4.9) comes from. Shot speed is a config value.
- **Missiles fly straight for now (decided, author).** Each flies a straight line in the world, from the launcher toward the robot (see "A strike from a single enemy" above), which keeps the view readable while the turret aiming is tuned. Weaving or curving missiles are a later option, for example as a trait of tougher enemies.
- Length and damage depend on the enemy (author): a standard enemy is about 10 seconds with fairly low damage; stronger enemies run longer and hit harder; hard cap of about one minute. Tune length through the number and spacing of missiles in a wave.

### 4.9 Armour arithmetic (starting values for the PoC config)
Author's brief: assume half the missiles are shot down; the player should survive a couple of attacks from a standard enemy.
- Correction (author): a single missile every few seconds is trivial to defend. The original sent a small volley of 3 to 5 missiles every few seconds. The pressure comes from more targets than can be handled at once, and choosing which to shoot first.
- Standard attack: about 10 seconds, 3 volleys of 3-5 missiles (4 on average), so 12 missiles. At half shot down: 6 hits per attack.
- Starting armour: 8 plates, each absorbing 3 hits (intact, cracked, badly damaged, gone), so 24 hits in total. This keeps the dashboard readable instead of showing two dozen plates.
  | Player skill | Shot down | Hits per attack | Attacks survived |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Struggling | 3 of 12 | 9 | 2 (dies in the 3rd) |
  | Average | 6 of 12 | 6 | 3 (dies in the 4th) |
  | Good | 9 of 12 | 3 | 7 (dies in the 8th) |
  | Perfect | 12 of 12 | 0 | Unlimited |
- Tighter option: 2 hits per plate (16 hits), where the average player survives two attacks and dies in the third.
- Repair: an armour drop (for example from a mine) restores about 2 plates (6 hits), so one drop undoes one average attack.
- Stronger enemies scale by larger or faster volleys and heavier missiles (more hits each), up to the one-minute cap. Upgrades are meant to add plates before the player faces a boss.
- **Attrition concern (author):** if each enemy gets off an average of one attack before being killed, the player needs an armour pickup for every enemy met, which is too harsh. Either the chance of the player striking first must be quite high, or armour pickups must be much better.
- Proposal: use both levers, leaning on first strike.
  - First strike is half-built into the rules: attacks cannot happen during a missile flight, so spotting an enemy and firing first means a hit kills it before it attacks. Chance of no damage is roughly (chance of seeing it first) x (chance the missile hits). Make the player's detection range on the short-range map comfortably larger than the range at which enemies engage, so an attentive player usually shoots first and damage is mostly the price of a miss or inattention.
  - Confirmed for the original (author): enemies are normally visible on the map before they fire, so first strike was usually available.
  - Engagement range as a third difficulty dial (author): standard enemies engage well inside the player's detection range; harder enemies have longer range or open fire earlier, shrinking or removing the first-strike window. Detection range can grow with upgrades, so the two race each other. A boss that out-ranges the starting robot guarantees an attack, which is fair for the endgame. Boss-tier countermeasures could also shorten the player's detection.
  - Expected cost per enemy at 2 plates per attack: 0% first-strike kills = 2 plates (4 enemies per 8 plates); 50% = 1 plate (8 enemies); 75% = 0.5 plate (16 enemies).
  - Better pickups: 3-4 plates each, covering roughly four to eight enemies at those rates.
  - Free softeners: patrollers have no missiles (manual), so not every enemy attacks; destroyed attackers could sometimes drop armour, making fights partly self-funding.
  - **Shield recharge (author finds this interesting: wait to get health back at the cost of energy, or the risk of being ganked?).** Agreed by the author, including the rule that only damaged plates recharge and lost plates need a pickup:
    - Resting is standing still while not threatened (the same "threatened" state used for saving), so no new icon.
    - Energy is the price: recharging drains the batteries at a visible rate. Damage therefore ultimately costs energy, and hauler cargo becomes the master resource.
    - The gank risk needs no extra rules: enemies patrol, so a stationary robot can be found, and an attack interrupts the rest.
    - Guards against turtling: only damaged plates recharge, lost plates do not (a cracked plate heals, a destroyed one needs an armour pickup), which keeps pickups valuable and caps resting; make it quick (a few seconds per plate) so the choice is about cost, not patience; make the energy cost bite so topping up after every scratch is not worth it.
    - Fits the energy pressure: standing around normally just wastes energy; now it buys something.
  - Tuning target: an average player loses about 1 plate per enemy encounter.
- **Arc damage model (decided, author; #82).** Replaces "the top plate takes every hit". The plates stand in a row across the robot's front arc, left to right, and each guards a slice of the bearings an incoming missile can arrive from. A hit cracks the plate facing it, and three hits break it. A hit on a slice whose plate is gone spills to the nearest standing plate (on a tie the stronger one, then the left one), so the robot still takes 24 hits in all and zero standing plates is still death. During an attack, the plate a closing missile will strike glows in the shield row, and the plate just hit flashes. An armour drop fully mends the two weakest plates, broken ones first. Why: with top-down damage, recharge could mend at most two hits, so the rest-or-risk choice barely existed. Spread hits now leave cracks that recharge can mend, and focused hits break plates that need armour. It also gives the defence mini-game a decision (shoot down the missiles heading for a weak plate first) and a new enemy dial (a focused volley breaks plates). Considered and rejected: a random plate per hit (the same recharge benefit, but no decision in the defence) and a separate recharging shield over permanent armour (a third gauge, and it undoes the agreed plates-and-cells display). Accepted consequence: the robot is more durable once recharge exists, so recharge's energy cost is the main tuning lever.
- **Recharge and threatened, as built (#82).** Threatened means the breather after an attack is running, or an alerted enemy (not destroyed) is within scan range (12 cells). Resting is standing still with time running (no chooser open), not threatened, with a cracked standing plate and energy above the low-power line. While resting, cracks mend one at a time, most cracked plate first (left on a tie), 1.5 seconds each, and each costs a quarter of a cell, drawn as it mends; a broken plate is never recharged. Moving or a threat interrupts it, and the crack in progress starts over. Recharge stops at the low-power line, so resting can never be what flattens the batteries. Shown on the dashboard by RECHARGING over the gauges, the mending crack fading out, and a calmer state-of-mind trace. Placeholders, tuned by playtest: one attack's spread cracks (about six) cost a cell and a half, close to an energy drop's worth.

### 4.10 Enemy behaviour and aggression
Author-direction: aggression (storming in guns blazing, standing off, or running away) is tunable and should fit the role. A hauler should be predominantly defensive; a patrol should do its job and investigate.
| Role | Stance | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Hauler | Defensive | Keeps to its trips and flees if threatened; fires only if cornered or attacked. Fleeing takes its cargo away, so chasing is a choice |
| Patroller | Investigates | Follows its beat; comes to look when something is wrong; has no missiles (manual), so on confirming an intruder it runs for help |
| Defender | Guards | Stays home until alerted, then attacks intruders near its dome; does not chase far |
| Hunter | Aggressive | Actively seeks the player within its network and closes in, firing |
| Bosses | Relentless | Roam until alerted, then head straight for the player |
- Parameters (all in config), each stance being a small state machine: approach (charge, hold at range, retreat); leash (how far from home or route); persistence (how long it chases or searches); break point (whether it flees when damaged).
- **One hit, one kill.** In the original every enemy dies to a single missile hit (author; unusual in games, perhaps part of the charm). Recommendation: keep it. A shot already costs about 30 seconds of skilled flying and a missile; the hit is the payoff and should not merely wound; a tougher enemy should be harder to reach (denser terrain, countermeasures, a smaller terminal target) and more dangerous (attacks, engagement range), not a bigger health bar; and it keeps first strike clean, since multi-hit enemies would always get an attack in. Possible exception, to decide later: the final boss as a duel of two or three hits with attack waves between, or one hit with the hardest flight in the game.
- Four enemy difficulty dials in total: volley size and pace, damage per missile, engagement range, and aggression.
- Link to first strike: a charging enemy shrinks the player's window, one that stands off gives time. Killing a patroller before it gets away to fetch help prevents the defenders coming: a first-strike reward that involves no damage.
- **Hunters are always looking (decided, author).** This is not a stealth game: a guard that walks past the robot sees it. A hunter that comes within 5 cells of the robot (its sight radius, just inside its engagement range of 6) is alerted, with the usual short delay before its first attack. Distance only: no lines of sight. The player should see first, but only partly does: the local map shows 12 cells left and right but only 5 up and down, about the same as a hunter's sight, so a hunter directly north or south can see the robot almost as soon as it appears on the map. The full map shows no enemies, only networks (section 4.6), so the first look always happens on the local map. Once spotted, the robot has 1 to 3 seconds before the attack. **Open:** how the player reliably gets the first look (a taller local view, a smaller sight radius, hunters' sight shown on the map, or a longer delay after being seen). A scan still gives the robot away, but **by chance, falling with range (decided, author)**: a hunter within 6 cells (the scan's "close" zone) is always woken; from 6 to 12 cells the chance falls steadily to zero at the ring's edge (two in three at 8 cells, one in three at 10). Each scan rolls once per hunter, reproducibly from the clock and the hunter, so scanning again near a town rolls again. Found in testing: a scan near a town's edge woke the neighbouring town's hunter as well, every time. With no status markers, the player learns whether a far hunter woke by watching whether it leaves its post.
- **No status markers (decided, author).** The map does not show whether a hunter has seen the robot or is searching. A first build drew "!" and "?" over hunters; both were removed. The player reads behaviour instead: a hunter leaving its post, walking toward the robot, or heading for a cut station.
- **An alerted hunter closes to its preferred range, then holds (decided, author).** A first build sent an alerted hunter straight to the robot's cell. That made no sense for an enemy whose missiles reach 6 cells, left it sitting on top of the robot, and left walking away as the only response. Now it approaches until it is within its preferred range (4 cells for a hunter) and holds there while it attacks. It never stops on a building, so it carries on through one rather than halting inside it. The preferred range is the aggression dial from the four enemy dials above: a hunter at 4 cells, a boss perhaps at 2, a cautious enemy at 6. The player can still choose to close in (a short strike, but faster inbound missiles); the hunter just does not force point blank. Considered: keeping a distance band (backing off when the player comes close), which would remove the player's option to close in; and repositioning between attacks, which may come later. With a held range, standing still no longer makes each attack harder; the cooldown and the counterstrike are the pacing.
- **Hunter stages:** on its post; **patrolling** (while its network's power is cut: rounds of the network's domes until the power returns, then back to its post, section 4.3); and **alerted** (closes on the robot and attacks). Seeing the robot from any stage alerts it. **A hunter guards its network; it does not go hunting, despite its name (author, #91).** There is no reason for it to leave: if the robot is in missile range it fires over the mountains, and if not it waits. An alerted hunter closes on the robot only over its own network's ground (the town's area): with the robot outside, it goes to the cell in the network nearest the robot and waits there, attacking across the edge, over high ground if need be, whenever the robot is in range (6 cells). It keeps the alert while the robot is anywhere in its network, or outside it but within its sight or range; once the robot is outside and beyond both, it drops the alert and goes back to its post. Inside its own network it does not lose track (decided while building #91: dropping the alert inside the network would let the robot shake it off on its own ground). Its rounds during a power cut and its rearming already stay inside its network.
- **Defender stages (as built, #68; guard area and speed are placeholders):** **asleep** in its dome (hidden: not drawn, cannot be targeted, does not look, and a scan does not wake it); **woken** by a missile hit on its dome, destroyed or deflected, it comes out at its post, the free cell beside the dome, already alerted. It moves in real time, but its usual 1 to 3 second wait before its first attack starts only when the player leaves the result screen (found in playtest: started at impact, the wait ran out while the result showed, so the defender always struck first, against this section's "a sharp player often strikes first"); **alerted** it closes to the holding range and attacks, as a hunter does; once out it keeps watch like a hunter, but only over its **guard area**, 12 cells round its post. It sees and chases the robot only while the robot is inside that area and breaks off and goes home as soon as the robot leaves it. The 12 cells cover the missile's reach across the local view, so a dome cannot be opened from outside its defender's reach. It carries the same magazine of 2 attacks and rearms at the nearest intact dome in its network (#102; it used to rearm at its own post). It never patrols on a power cut (#79 is open).
- **Patroller stages (as built, #66; all values are placeholders in config).** The hunter guards its network, so patrollers cover the ground between (author). **Placement:** the map is split into a grid of segments (3 across, 2 down), with 2 patrollers in each. Each walks its own loop of 4 waypoints spread across its segment (open ground the robot can reach, at least 8 cells apart, ordered round the segment's centre so the loop does not cross itself). **Beat:** it walks the loop at 2 cells per second. **Investigating:** a disturbance draws it to look: a scan whose ring reaches it (to where the robot scanned from), or a missile hit or power cut within 15 cells (to the free cell beside the building). It searches for 6 seconds, then goes back to the nearest point of its beat. **Running for help:** it is always looking, like a hunter (5 cells). On seeing the robot it does not attack. It runs at 3.5 cells per second to the nearest armed enemy (a hunter or a defender, not another patroller; no point bringing unarmed support, author) that is not destroyed, already alerted or off rearming, and that would fight where the robot was seen: inside a hunter's network or within missile range of its edge (#91: a hunter fetched for a robot further out would never come), or inside a defender's guard area (found in testing: a defender fetched from outside its guard area went straight back to standby). A defender asleep in its dome counts: the patroller goes to its door and wakes it. Within 1.5 cells, help is raised: the helper is alerted, and the call is recorded for game logic. If its helper is destroyed or alerted on the way, it chooses again. **Fighting:** it is lightly armed (author: otherwise it is impotent). Once help is raised, or at once if nobody can be fetched, it joins the fight like a hunter, leashed 15 cells from where it started fighting. Its attack is one volley, against a hunter's three, from a magazine of 2, and it rearms at the nearest dome. When the fight ends it goes back to its beat. Killing it before it reaches help means no help comes, as section 4.10 intends. **Start position and a quiet corner:** no patroller loop (waypoints or the walk between them) comes within 16 cells of the starting town's centre, so the game cannot open with a patroller walking past, and the player gets the first look. This costs the starting segment its two patrollers, so 10 of the 12 are placed. The robot starts out of sight of every hunter and every cell a patroller walks, and at least half the local view in from every edge of the map, so the view is never cut off (found in play: a start on the map's top row hid everything to the north). Fallbacks, in order: drop the patrollers' cells, then the edges.
- **Hauler stages (as built, #67; all values are placeholders in config).** **Placement:** one hauler per network. Its route is a loop of stops, each the free cell beside its network's power station, a mine or a dome nearest the town centre, ordered round the centre; it starts at the first. Stops are open ground the robot can reach, so a hauler can always be chased. **Route:** it walks the loop at 1.5 cells per second. **Fleeing:** it is always looking (5 cells, like every enemy), and the robot in sight, a scan ring that reaches it, or a missile hit within 15 cells is a threat. It flees to the open cell, within 10 steps over the terrain, furthest from the threat, at 2.5 cells per second (slower than the robot's 3, so running one down is possible but costs a chase). If the threat moves round so its goal no longer takes it away, it chooses again; with nowhere further away it stays put (cornered). It keeps away until 8 seconds pass with no fresh threat, then goes back to the nearest stop on its route. It never attacks, is never alerted, calls nobody and does not count as a threat for recharge. **Cargo:** rolled as its supplies: at least one energy (power pods) and some armour (raw material), never missiles. **Destroyed:** one missile hit, as every enemy. An enemy is now recorded as destroyed at the moment of impact rather than when the result screen is dismissed, so its wreck, and a hauler's cargo, lies where it was struck instead of a few cells on. The hit does not scare the hauler it destroys. **Not yet:** firing when cornered, replacement by domes (#70), and economy tuning.
- **Enemy speeds fit their stances (#117; placeholders).** In cells per second: defender 1.5 (heavy, built to guard), patroller 2 on its beat and 3.5 running for help, hunter 2.5 (aggressive, closes in fastest), against the robot's walk of 3. Walking away is always possible except from a patroller running for help, but it costs ground against a hunter. Before this every fighting enemy moved at 2.
- **Attack pacing (decided, author).** Found in testing: a hunter re-attacked every 12 seconds counted from the start of its last attack, and an attack lasts about 10 seconds, so the next one came about 2 seconds after the last ended. Standing near a hunter took the shields from 8 plates to 2 in about a minute: attack, attack, attack, with no chance to respond. The rule: **the player must always have a reasonable chance to launch a strike before the next attack.**
  - *Superseded by "Turn-taking" below.* **Cooldown from the end of the attack:** a guaranteed minimum of 12 seconds plus a random 0 to 6 seconds (seeded). The minimum covers a human noticing the attack is over, choosing Missile, putting the cross on the hunter and confirming (roughly 4 to 8 seconds once learned). The random part stops attacks feeling like a metronome; the minimum keeps a reliable window. Attacks are already held off during a missile flight, so a player who uses the window is safe; a missed or crashed shot means the hunter is likely ready again, which is the fair price of a miss.
  - **First attack after an alert:** a short random delay of 1 to 3 seconds, so a hunter does not open fire the instant the robot comes into range.
  - **Range shapes both strikes.** Outbound missiles take longer the further the target (intended from the start, not yet built): 10 seconds plus about 3 seconds per map cell, capped at 30, with the course shortened to match. Inbound missiles likewise: about 1.6 seconds from point blank to about 2.6 seconds from the edge of engagement range (6 cells), scaled smoothly. This gives the player a real choice: close in, and the strike is short and easy but its attacks leave little time; hold back, and its attacks are gentler but the strike is a long, risky flight; run, and once out of its network and its range it gives up (#91). Standing still is the worst option: an alerted hunter walks in, so each attack comes from closer and is harder.
  - *Superseded by "Turn-taking" below, which adds a magazine with a visible retreat.* **Enemy ammunition: not for hunters for now.** It is cheap to count but invisible to the player unless the enemy visibly does something when it runs out (for example withdrawing to rearm). The cooldown, the counterstrike window and the leash already give the pressure a way to end. Parked as a possible enemy trait (a hauler might carry only a couple of missiles; a boss never runs out), for the separate discussion of how inbound strikes vary between enemies.
- **Turn-taking: time stops while targeting, a short breather after each attack, and a magazine (decided, author).** With several hunters alerted, each kept its own 12-second cooldown but they took turns, so one attacked the moment another's attack ended. Rethinking it showed the 12 seconds mostly paid for the interface (stepping a cross across the map with arrow keys), not for skill. The new model:
  - **Time stops while a cross chooser is open** (Missile, Grid Disable and Move). A full pause: hunters, attack timers, the shield window and Grid Disable's cooldown all freeze, and the map dims with "TIME STOPPED". The fiction: the robot's targeting computer thinks at machine speed, and the arrow-key chooser is only how a human talks to it. The skill stays in reacting quickly enough to open the chooser and in flying the missile in real time. Pausing is not an exploit: nothing happens while paused, the player only gets time to think. Considered: slowing time instead of stopping it; rejected as less clear.
  - **A shared 3-second breather after every incoming attack** (counted after the attack summary), during which no enemy may start an attack. Enough to open Missile, after which time stops. **No breather after the player's own missile flight:** if an enemy is ready when the robot's attention returns, it attacks at once (after its warning beat). Fights become alternating turns: the enemy shoots, the player shoots, the enemy shoots, until someone dies or the player gets out of range. Passing on a turn is allowed, but the next enemy may then take it.
  - **No per-enemy reload.** Instead each hunter carries a **magazine of 2 attacks**. When it is empty it **retreats to rearm** at a free cell beside the nearest intact dome in its network (see #102 below when there is none), rearms for **30 seconds**, then returns to its post **no longer alerted**, so it has to spot the robot again. A retreating or rearming hunter does not notice the robot and cannot be woken by a scan. This answers the earlier objection to enemy ammunition (invisible to the player): the player sees the hunter break off and leave. It gives the pressure a natural end, a choice (chase it and strike it as it withdraws, or use the quiet for the real objective), and keeps running away viable. The magazine is a per-enemy dial: a hunter 2, a hauler perhaps 1, a boss unlimited.
  - *Was parked: destroying the dome a hunter rearms at, leaving it unable to rearm.* **Built (#102): enemies rearm only at an intact dome in their network.** Domes are the enemy's arsenal (section 4.4), so a ruin is no use and there is no rearming at a post. Hunters, defenders and patrollers alike: each withdraws to the nearest intact dome in its network that it can walk to, a defender included (it used to rearm at its post, which for a defender woken by its dome's destruction meant beside the rubble). If the chosen dome is destroyed on the way, the enemy chooses again. With no intact dome left in its network, it cannot rearm: it is **disarmed**, goes back to its post (a patroller to its beat) and stays there, harmless until destroyed. A disarmed enemy is never alerted again, never attacks, and is no help to a patroller fetching it; a disarmed patroller still runs for help, so it can still bring an armed enemy. Why: it gives flattening a town's domes a tactical payoff (its guards eventually run dry), and a defender whose network has no other dome is a one-magazine threat. Assumed, open to play: a disarmed enemy holds its post rather than keeping watch on the robot, and a patroller rearms in the network of the town nearest its beat. Domes are never rebuilt for now; replacement and supply-chain rules (#70) may change that.
  - Unchanged: the first-attack delay of 1 to 3 seconds after an alert, and range shaping both strikes.

### 4.11 Enemy point defence
Author's idea: the player has a defensive gun (working name: CIWS, as on ships); tougher enemies, perhaps enemy military installations, could have defences too.
- Fits one hit, one kill: tough targets are harder to reach, not tougher to kill. It also mirrors the player's own gun.
- Rule: never a dice roll. A random shoot-down after 30 seconds of good flying would feel terrible; the defence must be something the player plays against.
- Terminal-phase hazard: on a defended target the last few seconds add tracer fire sweeping across the view, which the player weaves through with the same left and right controls. Same skill, more intense finish, no new instrument.
- **Author's decision:** the terminal-phase hazard is the preferred form. The dashboard counter below is **not to be implemented for now; consider later.** Reasons: the author does not like switching from missile to cockpit and back, and if a boss must be hit with the EMP before firing, the game has to signal that somehow.
- Deferred: counterable from the dashboard: a powered defence is knocked out by the grid-disabler for the window, giving the EMP a second use and making "disable first, then strike" a plan. (The pre-launch jamming counter in section 4.1 is deferred for the same reasons.)
- Later option: arcs of fire, so the approach direction (where the player fires from) matters.
- Tiering, as with countermeasures: none on standard enemies; present on tougher enemies and military installations; heaviest on bosses. With jamming on the direction marker, a late target can be hard at both ends of the flight while still dying to one hit.
- Passes the four tests: creates decisions (dodge, or disable first); readable if tracers are distinct; fits in 30 seconds; appears only after the basic flight is learned.
- **As built: aimed bursts, not a sweep (author: "it shouldn't look like a windscreen wiper, should be some kind of point defence").** In the terminal phase a defended target's turret fires bursts at the missile. Each burst is announced by a muzzle flash on the target, and its aim is fixed at the flash: at where the missile is, leading a steady drift a little. After the flash, a short stream of rounds leaves the muzzle and flies straight down that line, swelling as it closes; one that passes the missile close enough destroys it ("SHOT DOWN", the missile spent, nothing destroyed). Bursts overlap, so the player keeps clear of every line of fire on screen and then comes back onto the target. Never a dice roll: no burst is announced close to the target, so the last can always be dodged, and the tests fly a pilot that dodges and always hits. Placeholders in config (`pointDefence`): first burst 0.5 s into the terminal phase, one every 1.1 s, a 0.35 s flash, 4 rounds per burst, and a ceasefire 160 units out. Which targets carry it is for escalation to decide (damage done, section 4.5); until then a feel-test toggle (`pointDefenceEverywhere`) defends every target, for tuning.

### 4.12 Technology (agreed by the author as a starting point; it could be reimplemented in different tech later)
- **TypeScript with the browser's 2D canvas, no game engine.** Wrap as a Windows desktop app later if wanted.
- Reasons: the game is lines, panels and state machines and needs no physics, 3D or asset pipeline; everything is code and text, which suits building with Claude Code; rules can be tested automatically and visuals checked with headless-browser screenshots; instant reload for feel-testing; canvas line drawing with a little glow gives the vector-display look.
- Structure: a simulation core with a fixed timestep and a seeded random generator (reproducible runs, maps shareable by seed); all tuning values in config files; three screens as a state machine (dashboard, missile flight, missile defence); an input layer mapping keyboard and mouse to the same actions; logic kept separate from rendering so a later port is easier.
- Alternatives considered: Godot 4 (conventional engine, built-in export and controller support, more than this game needs); C++ with raylib (native, good fit for the graphics, slower to iterate and harder to verify automatically).
- Trade-off: it runs in a browser first; on Windows that means opening a local page until it is wrapped.

### 4.13 Setting
**Concept [Author-direction]:** an installation found on another planet. It is not clear what it is, perhaps something like one of the closed "atomic cities" of the USSR, but we do not really know. We were sent to investigate.

**What that gives us:**
- No justification needed for the hardware. A sealed, self-sufficient, heavily guarded installation that is on no map can have weapons, factories, a power grid and guard machines without the player ever being told why. This answers the author's question of why the machine would have cruise missiles.
- The mystery is the reason to explore: the player was sent to find out what the place is, which is the same drive as finding the next upgrade, with a question behind it.
- The installation is still running: mines dig, haulers carry, domes build machines, machines guard the mines. An economy working flat out with nobody visibly in charge.

**Proposals that follow from it [Proposal]:**
- Names are the investigator's field labels. Nobody knows what the machines are called, so they are named by what they do (runner, hauler, guard, hunter), and buildings likewise (power source; structure, purpose unknown). These are the role names already used in this document.
- The catalogue is the investigator's field notes. It fills in as things are encountered and is how the story is told, with no cutscenes. (This replaces the original's Info icon.)
- The player's robot could be one of the installation's own machines, found on site and merged with, not brought along. That would explain why it has a will of its own and sometimes overrules the player, why it is armed, and why its upgrades are buried under the installation's buildings.
- Tone: deliberate, sealed-off, a little melancholy; not the original's war between two factions. Avoid the original's red-versus-blue colour identity.

**Open:**
- Who built it? People, in a lost or secret programme (closest to the atomic-city idea); unknown builders (stranger, and the machines need not make human sense); or never answered. Lean: never fully answered, perhaps a hint when the last piece is recovered.
- Who the player is, beyond "sent to investigate".
- What the six pieces add up to in this setting, and what the ending is.

**Alternatives considered:** a dead or unfinished mining colony (close to the setting of the 1985 game Tau Ceti; the premise is common ground and fine to reuse, but it leaves the weapons unexplained unless they are repurposed tools such as blasting drones); a colony security force; an openly military outpost.

**Title:** not chosen. Checked so far: "Terminal Phase" is already an existing space shooter (dropped); "Carapace" is used by several itch.io projects (crowded); "Blue Moon" is a place name in the original's backstory (avoid); "Nap of the Earth" turned up no game of that name in one search (candidate, not cleared). Choose the title once the setting is firmer, and check any candidate against Steam, itch.io and the trademark registers before publishing.

### 4.14 Enemy build plan (proposal, not yet agreed)
- **Why the hunter first:** the patroller has "defensive guns only, no missiles" (section 2.6), but the missile-defence mini-game starts on an incoming missile. Only the hunter (one per network, with guns and missiles) can start it, so slice 1 uses the hunter.
- **Slice 1: one hunter per network.**
  1. Hunter on the map: one per network, patrolling a beat between that network's buildings, shown on the mini map. It stays on its beat until alerted.
  2. Alert: a scan ring (section 4.2) that reaches the hunter alerts it, and it heads for the robot.
  3. Engagement: when in range, it fires 3 volleys of 3 to 5 missiles (about 12 in total) over about 10 seconds, and the missile-defence mini-game starts (sections 4.8 and 4.9).
  4. Damage: each missile that gets through is one hit. A shield plate absorbs 3 hits, and there are 8 plates (24 hits), as in section 4.9. Shields are the robot's own, so grid disable does not affect them.
  5. Kill: one missile hit destroys the hunter (section 4.10). It is not replaced in this slice.
- **Slice 2:** the patroller, which runs for help when it confirms an intruder, and the defender, which stays in its dome until alerted.
- **Slice 3:** the hauler, which flees and takes its cargo with it. Bosses come last.
  - Decided (author): no escort for the hauler for now. Revisit if playtesting shows haulers are easy prey.
  - Decided (author, #121): haulers carry **between networks**, not round their own town, which read as a small aimless circle. A trip loads at a power station or mine, and unloads at a dome in a neighbouring network: one whose walk from here passes through no third town. Each trip's destination is a neighbour with an intact dome left, never straight back where the last load came from unless it is the only choice, picked by a seeded hash of the hauler and its trip so it looks unpredictable but is the same every run. With no neighbour to take a load, the hauler waits at its pickup. Every trip passes through the wall gaps (section 4.7), which become places to wait for one.
  - Later, with replacement and supply (#70): the hashed choice becomes "the neediest neighbour", so haulers balance supplies and their routes shift as the player damages the economy.
- **Hunter behaviour (agreed by the author, section 4.10 stances):** a scan no longer starts an attack. It alerts the hunter, and the hunter becomes a live enemy with these stages:
  1. **Idle:** stays at its post, unaware.
  2. **Alerted:** a scan's ring reached it. It moves toward the robot.
  3. **Engaging:** the robot is within engage range. A short warning, then the attack, as above.
  4. **Chasing:** *superseded by #91 (section 4.10, hunter stages): a hunter no longer chases on a leash of about two towns. It pursues only within its own network, and fires across the edge when the robot is in range.*
  5. **Dead:** a missile hit.
  - An alerted hunter stays alerted until the robot is out of its network and beyond its sight and range, or it dies (#91). There is no timer for losing track.
  - Once it is dead it stays dead for good.
  - Clarified: a destroyed hauler leaves its cargo to be picked up with the scanner (section 2.6), so killing it is a reward. Fleeing is what takes the cargo away (section 4.10).
- **Decisions still open for slice 1** (placeholders until agreed):
  - Hits per plate: 3 (section 4.9), or 2 for the tighter option in 4.9.
  - Tuning target: section 4.9 says an average player loses about one plate per encounter. The table in 4.9 gives about two plates at half shot down, so this needs checking in playtesting.
  - Engagement and timers: the shield window keeps running during an engagement (decided).
- **Controls:** keyboard only for the defence mini-game, as for the rest of the game. The mouse option in section 4.8 is not wanted for now.

## 5. Proof of concept plan
- One network's worth: a handful of domes plus a power plant, one enemy type (a patroller), and the full missile loop (select, launch, guide, hit, drop, pick up). [Author-direction]
- Verbs: Move, Scan, Missile first; add the grid-disabler when shielded domes arrive. Piece collection is automatic.
- Hand-built map, so missile feel can be tuned without a generator moving the goalposts.
- Data-driven: buildings, drops and enemy placement in config, so generation can be added later without a rewrite.
- Prototype toggles for feel-testing: classic controls versus altitude bands; distance indicator off, marker change or audio ping; the scanner radius and cooldown.
- Grow in steps: more buildings, more enemy behaviours, more networks, then the full map.

### 5.1 First slice to build: the missile flight on its own
A single playable page, no dashboard or map yet. Done when all of this works:
- Forward-looking wireframe view: horizon line, mountain silhouettes, a direction marker along the top edge. Our own art; nothing copied from the original.
- Controls: left and right only, via arrow keys and via horizontal mouse movement.
- The marker starts centred, moves off-centre only as the player steers, and clamps at the edge when far off course. No wrap-around.
- Mountains: the visible silhouette is the hitbox. Generated from a seed, with a check that a passable path always exists.
- Flight lasts about 30 seconds (in config). Near the end, line art of the target appears and the player must fly into it.
- Three outcomes: hit (give this the polish), crash into a mountain (explosion), and miss ("missed" message). A key restarts with a new seed.
- Engineering: fixed timestep, seeded random generator, all tuning values in one config file, logic separate from drawing, and an automated test for the passable-path rule.
- Stub the feel-test toggles so they can be added next: distance indicator (none, marker change, audio ping) and altitude bands (off).

## 6. Open questions
1. **Scope:** a compact game or the full original structure (ten networks, six pieces)? [Author: start small, grow]
2. Fixed map per game, or generated? Deferred; the PoC uses a hand-built map.
3. **Missile restock (decided, #86):** a rack of 8, refilled only by missile supplies in ruins, with no floor for an empty rack (section 4.4). The original's method is still unknown.
4. Energy (one slowly ticking-down bank of cells): **empty is immediate death (decided, #15; section 3, item 18).** First version: it drains with time (a cell every 90 seconds) and with walking (0.02 cells per map cell); scanning, missiles and grid disable cost nothing; energy and armour drops in ruins refill it (section 4.4). All rates are placeholders for playtest. Superseded: Proposal: immobilisation, since it is cheap to build and death then follows naturally from enemy attacks, with a reprieve if energy is within pickup reach. It needs a guard against a soft-lock when nothing is nearby (a short timer to death, or the original's "abandon" control), a clear low-energy warning, and reliable refill sources (hauler cargo), or it will feel unfair.
5. What does destroying a beacon actually do in the original? The manual lists it as a valid missile target.
6. How large is the scanner's real range (the manual says "immediate vicinity", the author observes zero)?
7. Missile defence: starts automatically (section 2.9a); controls, enemy-dependent length (about 10 seconds for a standard enemy, never over about a minute) and the no-interrupt-during-flight rule are settled (section 3). Still open: how many missiles per attack, and whether the shield window and cooldowns keep running during an engagement.
8. Margin for error around mountains, and the length of a full playthrough.
9. Altitude bands: yes or no? Distance indicator: yes or no? (Both decided by feel-testing.)
10. Setting: concept chosen (section 4.13); still open are who built the installation, who the player is, what the pieces add up to, and the title and visual identity. (Engine and language are settled: section 4.12.)
11. Power station drops: stays indestructible with energy from haulers, or changes?
12. **Damage model** (partly decided): zero shields is death (author). Shields are shown as armour plates alongside battery cells for energy (agreed, section 4.6). Still open: how much damage each enemy's missile does, how repair works (armour from mines, restoration on upgrade), and whether damage degrades systems or only counts down. What death costs is settled (section 3, item 19). The original manual says a missile that strikes "damages or destroys" the robot, and saving was not possible while the robot felt threatened.

## 7. Deferred topics
- **Cluster overlap at mini-map scale** [Author-raised]: tested with four domes packed into about three local-map cells at 16 pixels. The icons merge into a blob, and the selected one is lost inside a cluster. Options: spread overlapping icons within their cells; show one icon with a count, and let the cross pick among the members; or enlarge the local view around the cross. Decide once clusters are placed by the generator, and check it with mixed building kinds too.
- Enemy attacks and the missile-defence mini-game: the author asked to return to this later. It has since been described (sections 2.9a and 4.8); remaining detail to settle: the damage model, missiles per attack, and timer interactions. A hard cap on simultaneous attackers protects this mini-game from overload.

- Dashboard counters to enemy defences, both deferred by the author: using the grid-disabler to knock out point defences before firing (section 4.11), and player jamming against enemy countermeasures (section 4.1). Reasons: no switching from missile to cockpit and back; any pre-firing requirement would need signalling; jamming a jammer sounds like an arms race.
